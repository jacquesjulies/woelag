Zinc: A Small Mineral With an Important Role in Women's and Men's Health

From immune function and skin repair to reproductive health, zinc quietly supports many of the body's everyday systems. Understanding what it does—and what low levels can mean—helps put supplements into perspective.

The key point: Zinc is essential, but taking more does not automatically mean better health. A supplement can help when intake is inadequate or a deficiency is present; it cannot guarantee stronger immunity, higher hormones or protection from illness.

What does zinc do in the body?

Zinc is a trace mineral needed for the activity of hundreds of enzymes and proteins. It supports normal immune function, DNA and protein synthesis, cell division, wound healing, taste and smell, and the maintenance of healthy skin. It also plays a role in normal growth and reproductive function.

Zinc and men's health

In men, zinc is involved in normal reproductive function, sperm development and the production of hormones including testosterone. A significant zinc deficiency can disrupt reproductive function, but this does not mean zinc supplements reliably increase testosterone, fertility or sexual performance in men whose zinc status is already adequate. Persistent concerns about hormones or fertility deserve medical assessment rather than self-treatment with high-dose supplements.

Zinc and women's health

Zinc is also important for women's immune function, skin maintenance, tissue repair and reproductive health. During pregnancy and breastfeeding, nutritional needs change, so supplement choices should be discussed with a healthcare professional. Zinc supports normal biology; it is not a treatment for menstrual problems, infertility, hormonal imbalance or pregnancy complications.

What can low zinc levels look like?

Possible signs of zinc deficiency include slower wound healing, changes in taste or smell, reduced appetite, hair loss, skin changes and more frequent infections. In children and teenagers, deficiency can affect growth. These signs are not specific to zinc and can have many other causes, so they should not be used to diagnose a deficiency by themselves.

People at greater risk can include those with certain digestive disorders or bariatric surgery, people with limited dietary variety, and some people following restrictive diets. A clinician can consider diet, symptoms and appropriate tests where needed.

Can correcting low zinc improve health?

If zinc intake is too low, restoring an appropriate amount can help normal immune function, skin repair, growth and reproductive processes. Zinc lozenges have been studied for common colds, and some research suggests they may shorten a cold when used early, but results depend on the preparation and dose and side effects can occur. This is not proof that a daily zinc capsule prevents colds or treats infections.

There is no sound basis for promising that zinc supplements will cure acne, stop all hair loss, prevent every infection, or raise testosterone in everyone. The clearest benefit is correcting inadequate intake while keeping total intake within appropriate limits.

Food sources of zinc

Useful food sources include meat, shellfish, fish, dairy products, eggs, beans, lentils, nuts, seeds and whole grains. Zinc from animal foods is generally easier to absorb; phytates in some grains and legumes can reduce absorption, although these foods can still contribute meaningfully to a balanced diet.

Making zinc part of a sensible wellness routine

For adults, the recommended daily amount is about 11 mg for men and 8 mg for women; needs differ during pregnancy and breastfeeding. The US adult upper limit is 40 mg per day from all sources. These are general reference values, not personalised dosing instructions, and local guidance or individual needs may differ.

This product's listed 22.5 mg of elemental zinc is a meaningful supplemental dose, so consider zinc from other supplements and fortified products as well. Long-term high zinc intake can cause nausea and interfere with copper status. If you take chronic medication, have an underlying health condition, are pregnant or breastfeeding, or are considering long-term use, speak with your GP or pharmacist first.

Our takeaway

Zinc is essential for both men and women, supporting immunity, healing, skin, metabolism and reproductive function. Correcting a deficiency can make a real difference, but supplements are not a shortcut to guaranteed hormone changes or disease prevention. Food, accurate product information and appropriate professional advice remain the foundation of a thoughtful choice.
