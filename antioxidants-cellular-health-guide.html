Antioxidants and Cellular Health: Separating Science from Hype

The word antioxidant often appears on wellness packaging, linked to energy, ageing and cellular protection. The underlying biology is real, but that does not mean every antioxidant supplement delivers the outcomes suggested by its marketing.

What are antioxidants?

Antioxidants are substances that can help limit certain forms of oxidative damage. The body has its own antioxidant systems, and many foods—including colourful vegetables, fruit, legumes, nuts and whole grains—contain compounds involved in these processes.

Why food patterns matter

A varied diet supplies many nutrients and plant compounds together. Researchers study how these dietary patterns relate to health, but it is difficult to translate findings about whole foods into a claim that one concentrated capsule will produce the same result.

Do antioxidant supplements prevent disease?

High-dose antioxidant supplements have not consistently shown the benefits that broad marketing claims imply. In some circumstances, high doses can cause harm; for example, beta-carotene supplements can increase lung-cancer risk in people who smoke or have certain asbestos exposures. Antioxidant labels are not proof of disease prevention.

How to evaluate a product

Look for exact ingredients and doses, a clear intended use, transparent quality information and realistic claims. Be cautious of language such as “detoxes every cell,” “reverses ageing” or guarantees to prevent disease.

Safety notes

Concentrated supplements can interact with medicines and may be unsuitable around cancer treatment or before surgery. If you have a chronic condition or use regular medication, check with a qualified healthcare professional before adding high-dose antioxidant products.

A practical next step

Use this guide as a starting point, then read the product label carefully. Product availability, formulations and prices can change, so verify details with the retailer before buying.
